# Build your own Thai medicine envelopes to test

No public image dataset of Thai medicine envelopes (ซองยา), pharmacy drug stickers (ฉลากยา), or Thai prescription slips exists today. Searches on 18 October 2026 across Hugging Face, Kaggle, Roboflow Universe, GitHub, Thai government portals, and Thai research papers found nothing with real dose instructions and answer labels. The closest real Thai images are a small "Medical" slice of **ThaiOCRBench** (CC BY-SA 4.0), which shows Thai medicine boxes and hospital signs but has no dose fields. The best published Thai benchmark, a set of 100 real prescription-label photos, was never released. This matters because Jenny's parser (drug name, strength, dose amount, unit, times of day, meal timing, as-needed, warnings) can only be scored on images whose correct answers are known. The practical answer is to **generate synthetic envelopes yourselves**, with the correct answers saved next to each image, then print and photograph a subset with the iPad. Public Thai OCR sets check whether Apple Vision reads Thai at all, and foreign drug sets add some camera-condition variety. The synthetic route also follows the project's "health data: synthetic only" rule under Thailand's PDPA, which every real Thai label would put at risk, because real labels carry patient names and hospital numbers.

## Zero public Thai envelope datasets, after searching 6 platforms

The search was broad. Hugging Face's dataset search returned **0 results** for "thai medicine", "thai drug", "thai prescription", "thai pharmacy", "thai pill", "medicine envelope", "ฉลากยา" and "ซองยา" ([HF search](https://huggingface.co/datasets?search=thai)). GitHub searches using Thai words returned 0 repos. The only Thai medicine-label hackathon repo found, "Anya / อ่านยา" from July 2026, holds **one test photo and no license**, so legally it is all-rights-reserved ([GitHub](https://github.com/smzey/hackathon-anya)). The Thai FDA's drug database on data.go.th is an XML text service with "license not specified", not images ([data.go.th](https://gdcc.data.go.th/en/dataset/web-service-drug-reg)). NECTEC and AI for Thai offer OCR services and an old 2012 character-image corpus, not label photos ([NECTEC](https://www.nectec.or.th/timeline/2012-thai-ocr-corpus)).

Thai researchers have built label readers, but none shared the pictures. Thetbanthad et al. tested AI models on **100 real Thai prescription-label photos** from public health facilities, taken on different phones under mixed lighting and angles. Their data statement says only that the data "are contained within the article", so there is no download ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765698/)). The same group wrote a second paper about automatically blacking out personal information on Thai drug labels to follow the PDPA ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/app15094923)). That paper is the clearest evidence that **real Thai labels contain patient names and HN numbers**. A 2025 University of Phayao app for elderly users reports **98.67% accuracy** with fuzzy drug-name matching and regex dose parsing, but it also links no dataset ([JAIT](https://www.jait.us/show-258-1756-1.html)). These papers are useful as targets to compare against when you pitch to judges. They cannot serve as test data.

Two gaps remain. Roboflow Universe blocked direct browsing, so a small, poorly titled Thai project there could exist. Kaggle was searched only through a search engine, without logging in. A ten-minute manual search on universe.roboflow.com for "thai", "ยา" and "ฉลาก" would close the first gap.

## ThaiOCRBench and TEMS test reading Thai, not parsing doses

Several open Thai OCR sets can answer one narrow question: does Vision with `"th-TH"` read printed Thai correctly from real photos? None of them can score the parser, because none label dose, timing or warnings.

**One dataset, two names.** The research notes list ThaiOCRBench under both `scb10x/` and `typhoon-ai/`. A check of the Hugging Face API on 18 October 2026 shows that `huggingface.co/datasets/scb10x/ThaiOCRBench` **redirects (HTTP 307)** to `huggingface.co/datasets/typhoon-ai/ThaiOCRBench`. Typhoon is SCB 10X's AI brand, so this is the same dataset, and `typhoon-ai/ThaiOCRBench` is the current address. The matching code repo is still `github.com/scb-10x/ThaiOCRBench`.

ThaiOCRBench has **2,808 human-checked samples across 13 tasks**, about 1.9 GB, ungated, and was accepted at IJCNLP-AACL 2025 ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/typhoon-ai/ThaiOCRBench); [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04479)). Most images are real photos, and the builders removed or covered faces, names and ID numbers by hand ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04479v3)). In a sample of the first 100 rows, **7 were tagged "Medical"**. Their answers include drug-packaging text such as "ยาแก้ท้องอืด ท้องเฟ้อ ยาธาตุน้ำแดง" and hospital text such as "งดใช้โทรศัพท์ขณะรับยา" ([HF viewer](https://huggingface.co/datasets/typhoon-ai/ThaiOCRBench)). If that rate holds across the whole set, there are roughly 150-200 Medical rows, but nobody has counted them. Many questions ask the model to read text at given coordinates, so you will need to crop that region before passing it to Vision.

TEMS is the cleanest set for scoring line-level accuracy. It has **5,000 cropped text images from 1,625 smartphone photos** of signs, menus and packaging, mixing Thai, English and digits. Each answer is written in the file name. It is CC BY 4.0 and was published in July 2026 by Mahasarakham University ([Mendeley](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/ntdmgksh9w)). The mix of English and Thai on one line looks a lot like "Metformin 500 mg" printed beside Thai instructions.

## Foreign drug and pill sets help only at the edges

International medicine datasets are bigger, but they cover the wrong documents. Manufacturer boxes and pill photos do not carry patient-specific instructions like "วันละ 2 ครั้ง หลังอาหาร เช้า เย็น". The most useful are two Mendeley sets by the same authors: **2,000 phone photos of 166 drug packs** and **3,900 photos of 150 packs**, both CC BY 4.0, full of real glare, flash burns, shadows and blur ([Mendeley 1](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/bsmy5jjysy); [Mendeley 2](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/bjy2svvmn8)). They have no text labels. To use them, you would hand-label 50-100 images with the English drug name and strength, which only tests that one line of your parser.

Taiwan's twinkle-ai/tw-drug-labels-vision is worth opening even though it is in Chinese. It has **44,663 drug leaflet and carton records with 17 structured JSON fields** (name, dosage form, strength, usage, warnings), under CC BY 4.0 ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/twinkle-ai/tw-drug-labels-vision)). It shows the shape your own answer files should have. The receipt benchmark CORD v2 (CC BY 4.0) is the standard pattern for scoring field extraction: each image comes with a JSON of named fields such as `menu.nm` and `total.total_price` ([GitHub](https://github.com/clovaai/cord)).

Pill-image sets matter only if Jenny later identifies pills by sight. NLM's C3PI/RxIMAGE has about **133,000 consumer photos** under ODbL, with a 0.5 GB sample zip as the easy starting point ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/computational-photography-project-for-pill-identification-c3pi); [NLM index](https://data.lhncbc.nlm.nih.gov/public/Pills/index.html)). NLM says the retired Pillbox data should not be used to identify real pills ([GCN](https://www.gcn.com/data-analytics/2021/05/nlm-pillbox-put-out-to-pasture/316051/)). MEDISEG has the friendliest license (8,262 images, CC BY 4.0) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10825)). These are US and UK pills, so they cannot recognize Thai generics.

## Real-patient data rules out four sources under the synthetic-only rule

The project's hard rule says health data must be synthetic. Four sources break it or might. The **Bangladesh handwritten set** comes from real hospital prescriptions, even though its authors say they removed identifying details ([Mendeley](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/zjjtptvn6f)). The **VAIPE** prescription images come from multiple Vietnamese hospitals, and how they were de-identified could not be confirmed ([VinUni](https://smarthealth.vinuni.edu.vn/resources/)). The **Thetbanthad 100-label Thai set** is real patient labels that were anonymized and never published; asking the authors for it would bring PDPA and ethics paperwork with it ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765698/)). The **Roboflow Prescription-OCR** set has a "P_Name" (patient name) class and nobody has checked its images. Leaving all four out costs you almost nothing, because none of them shows Thai envelopes anyway.

ThaiOCRBench is acceptable. Its builders removed personal information by hand, and the sampled Medical rows show packaging and hospital signs, not patients ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04479v3)). Still, look at each Medical image before you use it, and skip any that show a name or HN. Apply the same rule to your own work: every envelope you generate, print or photograph needs a **fake** patient name and HN.

## A three-layer plan you can run during the hackathon

**Layer 1: a synthetic envelope generator (hundreds to thousands of images, with exact answers).** Start from the Ministry of Public Health's private-clinic label standard. It lists 7 required parts (facility, patient and HN, drug name with the Thai generic name, directions, indication, warnings, expiry) and includes a full sample label that is already fictional: "CETAMOL (PARACETAMOL) 500 MG", "รับประทานครั้งละ 1 เม็ด", "ห่างกันทุก 6 ชั่วโมง เมื่อมีอาการ", with Buddhist-era dates ([MOPH PDF](https://mrd.hss.moph.go.th/mrd1_hss/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/%e0%b8%a1%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%95%e0%b8%a3%e0%b8%90%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%99%e0%b8%89%e0%b8%a5%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%81%e0%b8%a2%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%aa%e0%b8%b3%e0%b8%ab%e0%b8%a3%e0%b8%b1%e0%b8%9a%e0%b8%aa%e0%b8%96%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%99%e0%b8%9e%e0%b8%a2%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%9a%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%a5%e0%b9%80%e0%b8%ad%e0%b8%81%e0%b8%8a%e0%b8%99.pdf)). For real drug names and strengths, use **TMT (Thai Medicines Terminology)**. It is free after registration, comes as Excel files, and has strings like "zolpidem tartrate 10 mg tablet" that split neatly into name, strength and form ([HSRI open data](https://opendata.hsri.or.th/dataset/e85ad683-f5c5-4831-983a-1e6d077efe16)). A good first list is the six common chronic drugs from a Thai hospital study: allopurinol, amlodipine, enalapril, glipizide, metformin and simvastatin ([thaidj](https://thaidj.org/index.php/smnj/article/view/8006)). Then expand to 100-300 common outpatient drugs. Render with free OFL fonts such as Sarabun and Noto Sans Thai ([Sarabun OFL](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/ofl/sarabun/OFL.txt); [Noto OFL](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/notofonts/thai/main/OFL.txt)). Use HTML/CSS or Pillow with the raqm layout engine so tone marks stack correctly over vowels (in words like "ที่" and "น้ำ"). Then add damage with **Augraphy**, which simulates bad printing, folds and photocopy noise ([Augraphy](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.14558)). Write the answer JSON from the same random values that drew the image, so the answers are always exactly right. Include hard cases: all-caps trade names, "mg" next to "มิลลิกรัม", Thai digits (๑๒๓), "เมื่อมีอาการ", and pre-printed tick boxes for เช้า/กลางวัน/เย็น/ก่อนนอน. A September 2026 Thai OCR study found that **using many different fonts** was the factor that most helped synthetic data match real data ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03595)), so rotate through every Thai font you legally have.

**Layer 2: print and photograph 50-100 of them with the iPad.** No published capture method exists for medicine labels, so copy the idea behind Real5-OmniDocBench: photograph the same pages under fixed conditions so you can compare conditions fairly ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/JiangWenxiang/Real5-OmniDocBench)). Print at real size (about 8×5 cm). Shoot each label flat in good light, in dim warm light, with a flash glare spot, tilted about 30°, crumpled and flattened, curved around a bottle, and with a thumb partly covering it. Keep the file number so each photo links back to its answer JSON. This is the only layer that tests the real camera on the device.

**Layer 3: real Thai text checks.** Run Vision on TEMS crops and on ThaiOCRBench's Medical and Retail rows to confirm Thai reading works on real-world photos that you did not design yourselves.

**How to score.** Report two numbers, the same way receipt benchmarks do ([CORD](https://github.com/clovaai/cord)). The first is **character error rate (CER)** on the raw OCR text, after Unicode NFC normalization and removing whitespace, which is how a 2026 Thai OCR paper scored ([arXiv App. C](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03595v1)). The second is **per-field exact match**: is the drug name right, is the strength right, and so on for each field, plus a "whole envelope correct" rate. Before you compare, change Thai digits to Arabic digits, treat mg/MG/มก./มิลลิกรัม as one unit, and compare strengths as numbers (5 mg equals 5.0 mg). Report results separately for each photo condition, so you can tell the judges something like "glare drops meal-timing accuracy from X to Y."

**How to run it fast.** Vision runs from a plain Swift script on the Mac, and ready-made batch OCR scripts exist to copy from ([get_live_text.swift](https://alexwlchan.net/projects/get_live_text/files/get_live_text.swift); [mac-ocr](https://github.com/privatenumber/mac-ocr)). A local check on the team's M-chip Mac confirmed one key detail: **Thai is available only at `.accurate` recognition level, not `.fast`**. `VNRecognizeTextRequest` and `RecognizeTextRequest` both predate iOS 26, so both are safe for the Xcode 26 rule. Feed the OCR text into the **same parser source file** the app uses, so you test the real code. Compiling that file directly with `swiftc` next to the script leaves `project.pbxproj` alone. Adding a local Swift package to the project would count as a new dependency, so ask the team first. Thai support was checked only on macOS 27, and the Vision model on macOS 27 can differ from the one on iOS 26. Run `supportedRecognitionLanguages()` on an Intel Mac or the iPad, and re-run about 20 test images on the iPad itself to confirm the Mac results match.

## Conclusion

The missing dataset is an advantage, not just a problem. The best-documented Thai label reader was tested on a private pile of 100 real photos, and its answers were checked only for the drug name. The other Thai papers found share no test images at all. A team that can show per-field accuracy on hundreds of synthetic envelopes, broken down by glare, tilt and crumpling, and confirmed on printed copies photographed with the iPad, will have stronger and more honest evidence than those papers, without touching any real patient data. Your answer files and generator could also become the first open Thai medicine-envelope test set, which no one has published yet.

The weak spot to admit to judges is realism. Synthetic layouts match only the layouts you imagined, and real hospitals use different printers, fonts and stamps. One safe way to narrow that gap: invent envelopes that copy the layout of a family member's real envelope, with fake names, drugs and HN, then print them. That adds real-world layout variety without breaking the synthetic-only rule.
